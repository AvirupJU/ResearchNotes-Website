Streaming Diffusion Avatars

Avirup Dey

Working notes from two parallel projects on real-time avatars driven by distilled diffusion models — one on controlling them, one on making them converse.

Strategic Goals

A useful avatar needs to do three things well: render in real time, respond to direction, and hold a conversation. Recent advances in streaming diffusion models like distillation and block-causal generation have made real-time rendering increasingly practical. But giving these models precise control and making them genuinely conversational remain open challenges.

The two projects below tackle these problems from different angles. The first asks how much control we can add to an existing, highly optimised renderer without having to train it all over again. The second looks at what it takes to build an avatar that can listen, respond, and behave naturally in a conversation. In both cases, the goal is not just to make the streaming fast, but to push these systems beyond the uncanny valley.

  1. Control a distilled streaming renderer without redistilling it. Few-step students are expensive to produce and their teachers are often impractical to revisit. If new control signals (head pose, expression) can only be added by rolling back to the teacher and re-running distillation, controllability will never keep pace with the renderers. We explore what can be bolted onto a frozen student. We employ a masked injection strategy where the audio tokens act within a predefined lip mask, and the pose/expression branches act outside it. We probe the limits of this mechanism keeping the generator backbone frozen.
  2. Moving towards a full duplex conversational system. One-way streaming avatar is a rendering problem; conversation is a two-way problem. The avatar has to listen, decide when to take the floor, respond with speech and the non-verbal behaviour that makes the response legible, all inside a latency budget a human will tolerate. Previous works addressed speaking and listening individually and often resorted to a relay of ASR-LLM-TTS-Render which introduced corruptions and latency leading to "uncanny beahaviour". Here, we bypass the sequence: our diffusion renderer remains active throughout - while listening it receives non-verbal cues which decode into pose and expressions, and while speaking a Speech-to-Speech engine generates audio tokens on the fly which decode into the avatar's response.
Project 01 Expression: blocked Pose: controllable

Adding Motion Control to a Distilled Audio-Only DiT

Can head pose and expression control be added to a frozen, distilled, audio-conditioned student — without rolling back to the teacher and redistilling?

Method

We implement a one-step streaming diffusion framework for talking avatars. It generates video from one reference image, speech audio and a text prompt. Identity is anchored by the input image, used as the first frame. Audio conditioning uses a streaming speech encoder (Wav2Vec2) to produce per-frame embeddings. A fixed local-future sliding window with heterogeneous noise levels is jointly denoised, emitting one clean block per step under constant per-step cost.

After distillation: a new motion control branch and a masked injection strategy that buys post-hoc pose and expression control without rolling back to the teacher.

  • One-step sliding-window denoising. Local-future look-ahead with heterogeneous noise; emit one clean block per step.
  • Dual-anchor temporal forcing. Style anchor (RoPE re-index) + temporal anchor (reuse recent clean blocks) + anchor-audio zero-padding.
  • Two-stage streaming distillation. Offline ODE backfill + distribution matching.
  • Post-hoc motion control. A motion control branch grafted on after distillation, with masked injection routing audio to the lips and the pose/expression branches everywhere else.
  • Specs.
    • 40 ms/frame at 512×512
    • Runs on a 24 GB consumer GPU
    • Post-hoc pose control from a reference video

Results

  • Masked injection disentangles lip-sync from externally imposed motion. The mouth mask keeps the lips bound to audio while the expression and pose branches drive everything else. Lip-leak in cross-reenact setting falls from +0.51 (without mask) to +0.02 (with it).
  • The avatar still streams in real time with the control branches grafted on. 40 ms/frame at 512×512 on a 24 GB consumer GPU. The two branches add ~3.9 M trainable parameters to the frozen 1.3 B base (~0.3%) and enter as per-block additive residuals inside the existing forward, so the rollout is unchanged — one forward still emits one clean block.
  • Head pose tracks the reference video. Pose is estimated with EMOCA on the generated and the ground-truth video and scored as the Euclidean distance between them. Against the audio-only model the error is cut by more than half.

  • Expression transfer is still missing. Nothing available to condition on moves it. A driving video's emotion does not transfer (the override test below). A text prompt does not either — captioning the same render “smiling” versus “angry” moves 3 of 4 expression channels in the same direction, a nudge rather than comprehension, and the distilled student runs no CFG to amplify one. Audio moves the mouth and nothing else, leaving the rest of the face wearing whatever the reference wore.
  • Expression is anchored to the reference image by the style anchor — and the two cannot be prised apart. Hold audio fixed and vary only the reference still: non-lip channels stay near-constant (std 0.01–0.05, drift ≈ 0) at the anchor's configuration for the whole clip. Turn the anchor down and the expression does relax — but identity goes with it, ArcFace similarity to the reference collapsing 0.89 → 0.42 as generation destabilises. Per-block ablation finds identity redundantly spread across all 30 block-sinks and expression saturating with no knee, so no scalar separates them. What prevents drift is what holds expression transfer back: the mechanism that keeps identity steady across an unbounded clip is the same one that pins the face to the reference. Moving it needs a learned edit to the anchor pathway — an identity-only anchor plus an attention adapter on the blocks that read it — not more conditioning.

Project 02 Real-time Speech brain: external

A Conversational Avatar That Reads the User

A controllable one-way avatar is not enough. Can the avatar listen to a user and reply with both appropriate speech and the non-verbal behaviour that sells it — in real time?

Renderer
A block-causal diffusion-forcing talking head: reference portrait + driving audio → video, with an extra branch that ingests the user's audio and motion for reactive listening.
Speech brain
Moshi (full-duplex ~7 B speech model, Mimi codec) generates the avatar's spoken reply, which is handed to the renderer as its avatar-audio track.
Evaluation
RealTalk dyadic conversations, 3 subjects; per-frame speaker annotations used to select windows and gate audio per speaker.

The finding that shaped the system

The renderer does not speak on its own. It generates the avatar's non-verbal dynamics — head pose, blinks, reactive listening motion — from the user branch, but its lip and speech motion comes entirely from a separately supplied audio track. Driven with silence it listens and reacts convincingly and never utters a word. Everything below follows from having to source speech externally.

What worked

  • It streams in real time. Measured on one GPU: motion generation 11.7 ms/frame (85.7 fps) — 3.4× faster than the 25 fps budget — pixel decode 16.6 ms/frame, so a full speaker render runs at ~28 ms/frame (~35 fps). Moshi itself runs ~1.7× faster than its own audio frame rate. An earlier “12 fps” figure turned out to be the offline batch harness, not the model: cutting denoise steps 10→2 barely moved it, proving the diffusion core was never the bottleneck.
  • The avatar listens, then takes the floor naturally. Voice-activity endpointing with a minimum-run filter (≥ 0.32 s, so breaths do not count as speech), a 0.5 s pause, repositioning of the reply, and a barge-in guard that re-mutes on user speech. This cut the response gap on one subject from ~17 s to ~0.5 s.
  • Two avatars hold clean, balanced turns. An explicit floor token decides who speaks. The key insight: a full-duplex model answers in overlap while still the listener, and muting it loses that reply. Force-silencing the listener on both streams — audio tokens and the text inner monologue — makes it genuinely hold its turn. Result: overlap 0.000 with airtime split 28% / 27%.
  • Autoregressive scale drift, diagnosed and fixed. Over 30 s the avatar progressively zoomed toward the camera (1.40×, monotonic), worst during silent listening when the audio conditioning is zeroed and the motion branch free-runs — there is no anchor back to the reference scale. A training-free de-trend of the motion latent before decoding (τ = 4 s) flattens it to 1.01× while preserving lips and blinks. Acting in pixel space cannot work: a face that has zoomed in cannot be un-cropped.
Stage Measured vs. real time (25 fps)
Motion generation 11.7 ms/frame (85.7 fps) 3.4× faster
Pixel decode 16.6 ms/frame (60 fps) 1.5× faster
Speaker render (motion + decode) ~28 ms/frame (~35 fps) faster than real time
Speech model (one avatar) ~47 ms/step (80 ms audio frame) ~1.7× faster

Only the speaker waits on the speech model; the listener renders reactive motion with no audio dependency and never sits on the critical path. The per-stage numbers put a sub-second handoff within reach — though the streaming loop itself is not yet built, and the current code renders offline in batch.

Where it stops: the speech brain

  • Speech must be supplied externally. This is the renderer's design, not a bug we can tune away — and it makes the quality of the whole system hostage to whatever produces the speech.
  • The replies are not smart enough. After ~3–4 coherent turns each avatar drifts back to an assistant persona (“What can I do for you?”) and the exchange runs dry. This is not turn-taking and not the seed — we fixed both. It is a bot-to-bot ceiling: the speech model's brain is a fixed, unpromptable 7 B LLM, and with no human grounding and no system prompt, two of them relax into their trained register. Holding a persona across many turns needs a promptable or stronger LLM — i.e. unbundling speech generation into an LLM + TTS cascade — while keeping the floor-token turn-taking that already works.
  • The codec handoff costs articulation. Speech arrives as ~1.1 kbps codec tokens, is decoded to a waveform, then re-encoded by the renderer's own audio encoder to drive the mouth. That double-encode smears the features (cosine 0.87 vs clean) and cuts mouth opening ~30%, with occasional dropped syllables. Speech enhancement and super-resolution on the decoded audio were tested and do not recover it — detail the codec discarded cannot be restored downstream. The fix belongs at the handoff: a learned adapter between the two feature spaces, or a renderer that ingests the speech model's features natively.
